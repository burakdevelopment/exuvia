Exuvia
Agentic scientific discovery

Dying satellites leave aluminium dust in the stratosphere. Which experiment tells us if it harms the ozone layer?

Exuvia is an AI research lab. Its agents test hypotheses on a validated physics model and recommend the one real-world measurement that removes the most uncertainty.

The problem in four steps

Why a burnt-up satellite can matter for the ozone layer.

01

A satellite burns up

At 60–85 km it ablates. About 30% of its aluminium becomes Al₂O₃ dust.

02

Nanoparticles form

Their size is unknown: anything from 2 nm to 200 nm is plausible.

03

They sink to the ozone layer

Published models disagree 10×: 30 years or 1–3 years.

04

Their surface frees chlorine

ClONO₂ + HCl → Cl₂ on the particle, the chemistry behind ozone loss.

What Exuvia does

A complete discovery loop. Agents pick experiments; the solver produces every number.

Model in one paragraph

1-D high-latitude column from 10 to 92.5 km (33 levels, US Standard Atmosphere 1976) with 8 particle-size bins. It includes Stokes–Cunningham settling, residual-circulation descent, eddy diffusion and tropospheric removal, and tracks bare and sulfate-coated alumina separately. Surface area is S = 3C/(ρr) and activation is k = ¼·c̄·γ·S, compared with the background sulfate. The system is linear, so a run takes about 10 ms.

Limits

Indicators, not ozone loss. No 3-D transport or coagulation. Calibrated against another model, not observations. Lab rates are sparse (some measured at 296 K). Measurement values are design estimates with hypothetical outcomes.
